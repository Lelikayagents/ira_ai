LIBERTY
The exceedingly meagre development of the Roman 
law relating to private associations1  is particularly significant. Apart from craftsmen’s guilds and burial clubs, the  
number of private associations2 was certainly small. The  
fact that the Emperor Trajan refused to grant a concession to a voluntary fire brigade, although a serious fire had  
lately demonstrated the necessity for such an institution, 
and that the Governor Pliny, a friend of the Emperor’s,  
lent his support to the application3 shows how very rare  
must have been the grant of such a concession.4 It is true 
that the Emperor’s policy in regard to associations was  
dictated by political reasons; but that political timidity  
should have played such a permanent and decisive part in  
the matter is evidence that the Romans had very little  
sympathy for or joy in forming unions and societies. The  
great idea of concentrating capital by means of associations  
never occurred to them.5

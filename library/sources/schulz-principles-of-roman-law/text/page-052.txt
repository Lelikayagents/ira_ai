It is hardly necessary to illustrate this method, so characteristic 
of classical—non-elementary—literature. Let us observe, for  
example, a comparatively long and fairly complete passage from 
Ulpian’s Commentary on the Edict such as No. 614 of Lenel’s 
Palingenesia  ( =  D. 9. 2. 7. 1-8; 9; 11 pr. to 5).3  Here Ulpian 
wishes to show that the killing of a slave is only covered by the lex 
Aquilia  when it has been caused by physical contact of the killer 
with the slave. If death has been otherwise caused, then the law is 
not directly applicable, but the praetor shall allow a remedy by  
analogy. These rules are shown by the cases cited, but are not  
expressed. It is sufficiently surprising that Celsus went so far as to 
formulate the general rule given in § 6, which was ineffective  
enough, seeing that occidere is obviously a case of causam mortis  
praestare and not a contrast to it. The ensuing observations relative 
to the participation of more than one person, in which the question 
of contributory negligence is also discussed, are again strictly on 
casuistic lines. The compilers may, in this passage, have left out  
one or other of the cases contained in the original, but they are  
certain not to have left out theoretically stated principles, of which 
they were so inordinately fond; these were not contained in the  
original.
The post-classical era looked more leniently on abstract 
formulae, and many an abstract theoretical rule in the  
texts of our classical jurists originated in the later period.

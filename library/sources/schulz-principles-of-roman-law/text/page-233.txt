The quaestor’s relationship to his consuls or his governor  
was one of fidelity, and it by no means ended with the  
term of office ;* it was a very close bond1  2—Cicero says, like 
that of a child to its father3—the quaestor could not take  
offence, was expected to serve and support his superior,  
was not allowed to appear as a witness against him and  
so forth.4
Finally, private friendships founded permanent relationships of fidelity, of which our sources present a  
particularly clear and colourful picture.5 These sources,  
it is true, describe only the amieitia  of the higher social  
classes, but it is permissible to draw from this conclusions  
as-to the- amieitia  in Roman life in general. The Roman  
amieitia,  the foundation and dissolution of which was not  
unattended by forms, gave rise to a number of serious and  
very real duties. Roman friends made mutual claims on  
each other which would in many cases cause a modern

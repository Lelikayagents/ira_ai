Pupillary substitution3 is only comprehensible among  
people who consider intestate succession to be almost a  
misfortune; should the pupillus die intra pubertatem,  i.e.  
before he has attained the capacity to make a will, the  
father may make a testament for the pupillus . Roman  
jurisprudence treated the highly original productions of  
Roman testators and the clauses—often strange and confused—-of their wills with tender understanding. When  
it is remembered that even Justinian’s Digest contains an  
enormous bulk of material (a great deal of which has still  
to be investigated) relating to the interpretation of wills,  
some idea may be gained of the volume of classical literature devoted to the subject; it is highly characteristic of  
the Romans to have thus overestimated the importance 
of such extremely individual problems. Again it is the  
principle of liberty which caused the rejection of the  
contract of succession,4 though a transition from the  
mancipatio  (to the familiae emptors)  to the contract of  
succession would have seemed natural.
Thus Roman private law is in very truth a monumentum 
aere perennius  of the Roman principle of liberty*

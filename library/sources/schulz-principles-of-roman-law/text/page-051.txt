Most of the ‘school rules5  too, those epigrammatic statements handed down by classical writers as regulae veterum,  
arose in this period. Too much importance should, however, not be attached to the results of this ‘maxim-jurisprudence5 , as it has been called.1  2 On the whole only very 
elementary rules were at that time rendered in abstract  
formulae, and in classical times these efforts were only  
carried on with great reserve.3 In the elementary introductory works of classical times, to which the libri ires  
iuris civilis  by Sabinus also belong, the abstract method  
was followed; but apart from these the traditional casuistic  
method was employed. The large collections of responsa, 
quaestiones, and epistolae contain—more or less abridged  
and transformed—-the cases with which the author has  
dealt as legal adviser or teacher; there is no connecting  
theoretical text. The greater works too, which we are  
accustomed to call ‘Commentaries5  on the edict or on the  
libri iuris civilis  of Sabinus, particularly the digesta of  
Julian, the libri ad edictum  and ad Sabinum  of Pomponius,  
Paulus, and Ulpian, are casuistic in a peculiar way. They  
do not—as in a modern commentary—illustrate abstract  
principles by means of true or fictitious cases; rather does  
the work consist of a series of cases in which the legal rule  
occurs, but is not abstracted from them in a formula. The

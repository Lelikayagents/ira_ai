# Fritz Schulz, *Principles of Roman Law* — holdings

Page numbers are printed book pages. Each fragment is a slice of the same scan and has
its own offset: fragment 1 starts at printed page 40, fragment 2 at 140, fragment 3 at 223.

Only the chapters marked **[held]** are in the library.

## IV. Abstraction — pp. 40–65 **[held]**

| Pages | Section |
| --- | --- |
| 40 | Chapter opening; epigraphs (Javolenus, D. 50. 17. 202; Goethe). The case as the starting point of all law; abstraction as what every decision implies |
| 41 | Roman reserve towards abstraction; it is a considered position, not a primitive incapacity |
| 41–43 | First manifestation: private law seen through its remedies (*actio*, *exceptio*, interdict, *in integrum restitutio*); comparison with medieval English law (Maitland); the limits of that observation |
| 43–48 | Second manifestation: the avoidance of juristic definitions — conceptions without a term, terms without a definition, and the weakness of the definitions the sources do give (*dolus*, the parts of the formula, marriage, legacy, *obligatio*, *furtum*) |
| 48–53 | Third manifestation: the avoidance of abstract legal rules — pedantic enumeration in the *leges* (*lex Quinctia de aquaeductibus*, *lex Cornelia de sicariis*), a parallel from English statutes, and the casuistic character of juristic literature; Q. Mucius and Servius; the *regulae veterum*; the post-classical *regula* |
| 53–57 | Fourth manifestation: indifference to system — Q. Mucius, the Sabinus system, Gaius' *Institutiones*, the edict system, the Digest system, the *distinctiones* |
| 58–62 | Ulpian, *ad edictum* (D. 9. 2. 7. 1–8; 9 pr.–4; 11 pr.–5) reprinted in Latin with a facing English translation |
| 63–64 | Analysis of that passage: three sections, and where Ulpian's arrangement breaks down |
| 64–65 | Post-classical systematization; Cicero's contrary ideal (*in artem redigere*) and the defence of the casuistic method |

## VIII. Liberty — pp. 140–163 **[held]**

| Pages | Section |
| --- | --- |
| 140 | Chapter opening; epigraphs (Cicero, *in Verrem*; Byron). *Libertas* as a bounded conception: not free is whoever has a *dominus* |
| 141–146 | Liberty in constitutional law — *regnum* rejected, the Principate as no Dominate, *civitates liberae*, the peregrine communities, and the slow decline of civic self-government (Pliny's reports to Trajan) |
| 146–151 | Individualism in private law — husband and wife, the poverty of the law of private associations, *societas*, coheirship |
| 151–155 | Ownership: the conception is not the individualistic part, the law of ownership is; limits, duties, protection, *usucapio* |
| 155–157 | Obligations (solidary debts avoided) and succession (the will as the normal case, *lex Falcidia*, pupillary substitution, no contract of succession) |
| 157–158 | Mommsen's 1848 speech; none of the institutions of modern capitalism come from Roman law |
| 158–159 | The counterweight: *pietas*, *fides*, *humanitas* — the *officium* and the ban on abuse of right |
| 159–163 | The individual and the State — no totalitarian polis, expropriation almost unknown, public gifts freely given, and the absent legal guarantee |

## XI. Fidelity — pp. 223–238 **[held]**

| Pages | Section |
| --- | --- |
| 223–224 | Chapter opening; epigraphs (Valerius Maximus; Cicero, *de officiis*). *Fides* as being bound by one's own word, and the Romans' boast of it |
| 224–225 | What *fides* did to the law: the early recognition of the informal act-in-the-law — consensual and real contracts, *pacta praetoria*, *fideicommissum*, and the post-classical retreat from formalism |
| 225–227 | The concluded contract binds: no right of withdrawal for breach, no notice in hire and lease, *ius paenitendi* as post-classical |
| 227–228 | *Bona fides*: from keeping one's word to acting as honest people do |
| 228–229 | Fidelity to oaths, with Cicero against the enforced-oath excuse |
| 229–231 | Two rules of the doctrine of legal sources: the magistrate is bound by his own edict, and legal rules have no retroactive force |
| 231–233 | Standing ties of fidelity outside contract — freedman and patron, patron and client, contracts of hospitality, the quaestor and his superior |
| 233–236 | *Amicitia*: its duties, Cicero's letters, and the case of Metellus shielding Verres |
| 236–238 | What the ties explain in Roman law — mandate and *negotiorum gestio*, personal security and *cautiones*, the *consilium*; 'Roman individualism' is a legend |

Celsus, ‘nihil aliud est actio quam ius quod sibi debeatur iudicio 
persequendi’, is assuredly not classical (it is, e.g., not applicable to a 
vindicatio  or to the praeiudicium,  Gaius, iv. 44).1  And yet the  
Byzantines obviously discovered nothing better2 and were obliged 
to make use of it both under the Digest title de obligationibus et  
actionibus  (D. 44. 7. 51) and in the institutiones title de actionibus  
(4. 6 pr.).—Nor is there a definition of ownership (dominium).  The  
oft-quoted definition ‘Ownership is the right over a thing, by which 
the person entitled can dispose of the thing at his own discretion’, 
will be sought in vain in Roman sources.3—Nor do our sources  
contain definitions of dolus (except the actio de dolo), culpa, contractus, servitus, dos, heres, potest as, manus, Imperium, See.,  probably 
because the classical writers never attempted to find them.
Our sources do nevertheless provide us with definitions  
for certain conceptions. These are often very imperfectly  
worked out, owing no doubt in part to their authorship by  
post-classical writers. But even where undoubted classical  
definitions are found they show that definitions were not  
the forte of the Roman jurists.
Cicero tells how dolus (in actio de dolo)  was defined by its creator 
Aquilius Gallus: ‘cum ex eo quaereretur, quid esset dolus malus, 
respondebat, cum esset aliud simulatum, aliud actum’; i.e. he  
defined dolus as malicious misrepresentation, malicious deception.4

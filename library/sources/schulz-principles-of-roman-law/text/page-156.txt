The law of succession, finally, was also individualistic  
in character. Statutory rules of succession were of minor  
importance in Roman eyes; they were significantly described as the law of intestate succession, i.e. they were  
only applied in cases when there was no testamentary  
succession, the latter being considered the normal case.  
The Roman felt that succession by testamentary disposition, i.e. by individual will and not by typical regulation  
as in cases of intestacy, was an actual need. Cato, it is  
said, reproached himself with three things in his life, one  
of them being that he once lived for a whole day without  
having made a will;4 this anecdote, true or false, shows  
better than any other evidence how usual it was to make  
a w ill even as early as in the second century b .c.;5 to make 
a will was as much the duty of every self-respecting Roman  
as to keep proper accounts.6 The generous freedom of 
disposition accorded to a testator is in keeping with this  
principle; not till the beginning of the second century

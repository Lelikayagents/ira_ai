The relationship based on fidelity between patron and  
client was in the period under consideration extra-legal.2  
It sometimes happened that a whole community belonged  
to a patron’s clientele.3 It was usual for a relationship of  
this kind to arise between a community and the statesman  
who had been instrumental in joining it to the Roman  
Empire.4 The fidelity was hereditary and bound the  
parties to mutual support. The patron, for example,  
was not expected to appear as a witness against his  
client,5 the patron of a community was in duty bound to  
support that community’s interests to the best of his  
ability in Rome; from time to time the Senate would  
perhaps entrust him with the drafting of the municipal  
law for such community or with the giving of an arbitral  
award.6
Contracts of hospitality7 between two or more individuals, between individuals on the one hand and municipalities on the other, or between two or more municipalities, were of course concluded primarily with a view to  
providing for the guests’ physical needs; but over and  
above this also to conducting business for him, to his  
legal protection, in fact on a general basis of fidelity.

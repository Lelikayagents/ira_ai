In the face of the liberal nature of Roman private law it is not  
superfluous to stress the fact that none of the characteristic legal  
institutions of modern capitalism are derived from Roman law,  
such institutions being annuity bonds, bearer securities, shares,  
bills of exchange, trading companies (in their modern, capitalistic  
form), mortgages (as capital investment), direct agency.1’
The liberty thus accorded in private law does not,  
however, mean that within the limits of private law everybody could do as he pleased. Quite apart from the  
restrictions of public law, those imposed by fietas, fides,  
humanitas  (in short by the officium) were exceedingly real  
and powerful.2 Even though the general rule ‘male nostro  
iure uti non debemus’ may not have been formulated till  
post-classical times,3 it was already recognized in the  
republican and the classical era that abuse of the law

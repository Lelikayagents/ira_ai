ABSTRACTION
‘Classical writers treat private law from the point of view 
of procedure’ is inexact; on principle, they do not, as we  
have already seen,1  deal with the law from the point of 
view of a judge taking evidence. Classical jurisprudence  
does not contemplate the law of procedure as such, but  
the actiones. In wide fields of law legal science is restricted  
to the legal remedies propounded in the edict, particularly the actiones. The conditions under which the magistrate may allow the proposed remedies and the results to  
be expected from them are discussed; proposals are made as 
to the conditions under which the magistrate should grant  
legal remedies not propounded in the edict, particularly  
actiones utiles. The question —to give an example—-as to  
whether a debtor owing money is bound to pay interest  
in case of default is not discussed, but in treating the actio 
venditi  (where the question ‘quid venit in indicium’, 
i.e. what the iudex may and should decide is debated)  
the debtor’s obligation to pay interest in case of default  
is included. With this attitude medieval English jurisprudence, characterized by Maitland as follows, may be  
compared:2
‘Legal remedies, legal procedure, these are the all-important  
topics for the student. These being mastered, a knowledge of  
substantive law will come of itself. Not the nature of rights but  
the nature of writs3  must be his theme . . so thought our forefathers.’

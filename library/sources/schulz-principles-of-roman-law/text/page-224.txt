very truth they strive after fidelity; faithlessness is in their 
eyes a social blot. Fidelity is only one aspect of constantia 
which is to the Romans a man’s central virtue:1  ‘take a 
course and stick to it’. They become emotional when they 
speak offides. Cato as an old man lays stress on the fact 
that the Roman ancestors built the temple to Fides on the 
Capitol as near as possible to Jupiter, the greatest and best 
of the gods.1  2 Cicero declares fidelity to be the ‘most 
sacred thing in life’,3 Valerius Maximus says that it is the 
‘security for all human happiness’. Valerius was right 
when he declared that the whole world was acquainted 
with Roman fidelity. The author of the first book of the 
Maccabaeans knew that ‘the Romans kept amity with 
their friends and such as relied upon them’,4 while the 
author of the Gospel of St. John makes even Pilate keep 
to his word: ‘What I have written, I have written.’5
Roman law owes to the principle of fidelity a number 
of particularly characteristic features.
In the first place the early recognition of the informal 
act-in-the-law6 is significant. Fides demanded that a man 
kept his word, no matter in what form it was given. The 
four so-called consensual contracts—sale, hire (to which 
the contract of work and labour also belonged), partnership, and mandate—-were already known in republican

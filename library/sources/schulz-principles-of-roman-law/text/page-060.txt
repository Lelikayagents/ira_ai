But if a person has pushed a slave 
from a bridge, Celsus says he will be 
liable under the lex Aquilia,  without 
any distinction whether the slave was 
killed by the blow itself, or had been at 
once submerged, or had perished from 
having been overpowered by the force of 
the stream; in the same way as if some 
one had pushed a slave against a rock.
Proculus says that if a surgeon has 
been wanting in skill in operating upon 
a slave, either an action under the contract of hiring and letting, or under 
the lex Aquilia,  will be applicable.
If a midwife has given a medicine 
and the woman-slave has died in consequence, Labeo makes the following 
distinction: if the midwife has administered the medicine with her own 
hands, she is considered to have ‘killed’ 
the woman; but if she had only offered 
it, so that the woman might take it herself, an actio in factum must be given. 
This opinion is correct, for [in the second 
case] the midwife has but produced 
the cause of the death and not killed.
If by force or persuasion one person 
has administered medicine to another, 
either into his mouth or by giving him 
a clyster, or if he has anointed him with 
poison, he will be liable under the lex 
Aquilia just as the midwife who herself has administered the medicine.
If a person has killed a slave by starvation, Neratius says that he will be 
liable to an actio in factum.

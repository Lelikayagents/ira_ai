It is undoubtedly true that the predominantly casuistic  
method made the law and legal science difficult and  
obscure.3 But was not nevertheless the method of the  
classical writers, if not the more scientific, then the ‘more  
juristic5  way? Did they not here again show that they  
were thoroughbred jurists?4 One thing is certain: the  
casuistic method, which revealed a law dynamic and  
struggling,5 gave to classical literature a vivacity and freshness which could never have been attained by a strict  
theoretical system; to this method Roman juristic writings  
owe their peculiarly stimulating power, the encouragement to active co-operation which is produced by their  
concealment of a leading principle—in short just that  
attribute which has always excited the fascinated admiration of juristic posterity.

The above demonstrates that Ulpian, though attempting a 
systematic arrangement of his subject, did not succeed entirely in 
throwing off the haphazard method of stringing his cases loosely 
together.1
In post-classical times these strivings after a system with  
their distinctiones  and their categories were continued,1  2  
but here again success in building up a system was not  
attained. On the contrary, the final development resulted  
in a complete renunciation of systematic continuity. The  
collections of constitutions arrange them chronologically  
under the various titles. In Justinian’s Digest the writings  
from which extracts are taken are collected in heaps and  
groups and this constitutes the order of the Fragments.3  
A plan for law books of this kind could only have originated  
with persons accustomed to confine themselves to individual phenomena and to attach no value to systematic  
classification; again and again we see how thoroughly  
Roman Justinian’s legislative work is.
Cicero correctly describes this fundamental attitude  
of the Roman jurists—their dislike of abstractions and  
their generally unsystematic and casuistic method of work

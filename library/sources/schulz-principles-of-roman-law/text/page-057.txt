Nor do the greater systematic works of the second  
century a.d. show much concern in regard to their arrangement. The principal work of the second century and of  
Roman jurisprudence as a whole, the Digest of Julian,  
is compiled in the convenient leisurely fashion just described. Compare for this the longer connected passages  
in Justinian’s Digest;1  2 there is no clear arrangement in  
the modern sense, no progression according to a prearranged plan. There are no transitional passages showing  
the author’s scheme. It is true that here we must assume  
that the Compilers have made cuts, still in such long  
passages the plan of the whole should be visible; moreover,  
the Compilers would certainly have preserved passages  
showing an underlying system. In the third century  
systematization seems to have made some progress; the  
longer connected passages in Ulpian’s and Paulus’ Edict  
Commentaries bear traces of an improvement in this  
direction. Nevertheless the arrangement leaves much to  
be desired.
